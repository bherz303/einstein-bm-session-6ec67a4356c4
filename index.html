Why change the format

The last several BM sessions have been quiet. The managers are vocal offline and silent in the room, and when a problem surfaces they hand it up rather than solve it. That's the thing worth fixing — not the agenda.

Pair-first attacks all three at once. Everyone speaks inside the first ten minutes, but to one person instead of a room. Then each manager presents their partner's points, which is a far easier thing to do than advocate for your own — you're reporting, not exposing yourself. And because the pair has to hand something coherent to the room, they work the problem between them before leadership ever hears it.

Leadership speaking only at the close is the other half. The moment a regional answers first, the room goes back to receiving information.

The premise

TAKEN AS DONE

Every manager is trained. Every mover is enrolled. The app has been live for a week.

No status updates, no completion percentages, no branch-by-branch review. That's finished business and it shouldn't take a minute of this meeting. The question on the table is what the first week of real use actually looked like.

What managers bring

POSTED IN ADVANCE

Three items, written down, on the training app only. They bring these to the room — the notes are the ticket in.

One win

Something the app made better, or something you did that got a crew genuinely using it. Name the crew, name the day.

One loss

What's breaking in practice. Friction on the truck, confusion, workarounds people invented, what your movers complain about.

Opportunity or constraint

The one thing that would make it stick — or the one thing standing in the way.

Run of show

10 MANAGERS · 75 MINUTES
2:305 min

Open and pair off

Frame it in two sentences: one topic, everyone presents, leadership talks at the end. Say out loud that the session is being recorded and run through AI analysis afterward.

Pairs are pre-assigned — read them out and send them off.

LEADERSHIP
2:3510 min

Pairs brief each other

Five minutes each way. You are not swapping complaints — you are taking notes you'll have to deliver accurately in front of the room. Clarifying questions only.

The shared doc goes live the moment pairs break off. You type your partner's three points as bullets into your section while they talk. If they're giving you more than you can keep up with, they paraphrase until it's typeable — that compression is their job, not yours.

If you land on the same constraint, work it rather than logging the match — what you build together can replace the opportunity you brought or sit next to it, marked built in the doc.

Leadership stays out of the pairs entirely, but watches the doc: an empty section means that pair isn't finished. That's the completion signal, and it means nobody has to interrupt a conversation to ask.

MANAGERS, IN PAIRS
2:4550 min · 5 min each

All ten present their partner's notes

Timer on the screen, hard stop at five. Win, loss, opportunity or constraint — in that order, so the room hears something working before it hears something broken.

The partner gets fifteen seconds at the end to correct anything misrepresented. That single mechanic is what keeps people listening properly during the pairing.

When a point has already been made, name the match and add only the difference — "same constraint Rance named, ours shows up on two-truck jobs, not singles" — then move on. Thirty seconds, not five minutes. Nobody gives up their turn, and the room still hears the corroboration.

Leadership takes notes and does not respond. Questions get written down, not asked. They also keep a running count in the doc: a point landing for the third time is flagged systemic, and those flags are the debrief shortlist.

MANAGERS, TO THE ROOM
3:3510 min

Leadership debrief and close

Short. Name the two or three themes that showed up in more than one branch, say what happens to them, and commit to a date for the follow-up. Then break.

No decisions invented on the spot — the value of this meeting is the recording and what gets built from it.

LEADERSHIP
WORTH DECIDING BEFORE 9/3

Fifty minutes of consecutive presenting is the one place this sags — attention goes somewhere around presenter six or seven. It also puts the meeting at 75 minutes, past the under-an-hour bar this format was meant to hold.

Four minutes each puts you at 65 minutes; three puts you at 55. Three items in three minutes is tight but real, and a tighter clock usually sharpens the delivery rather than thinning it.

How the pairs get built

  • Pre-assigned, never self-selected. Left to choose, people pick who they already talk to and the whole point evaporates.
  • Never same branch, and cross-region wherever possible. A different rollout experience, no shared local politics, and it builds the peer network across regions that doesn't currently exist.
  • Rotate every session. Same pairs twice and it calcifies back into the comfortable conversation.
  • Odd number makes a trio, not a leadership pair. Putting a regional in a pair breaks the one rule the format depends on. A trio runs four minutes a leg instead of five.
  • A matching constraint is working material, not a dead end. If you both land on the same wall, don't just note the match — work it for the time you have left. What you build can replace the opportunity you walked in with, or sit alongside it. Two people who hit the same wall should leave with something neither of them brought.
  • Mark anything built in the pairing as built in the doc. One word next to the item. It's the only way to tell whether this format is producing new thinking or just relaying what people already had — and that is the thing to judge it on after the first run.
  • If you match on all three with nothing new to build, you are still not finished. Spend what's left finding one thing you would each do differently in the other's branch. Otherwise agreement becomes the moment the conversation stops.
  • Bullets in the shared doc, typed live, nothing before the meeting. Ten sections named in advance and kept dark until pair-off, so nobody drafts for an audience and nobody hunts for where to type. Bullets only — a full write-up on a screen everyone has already read turns the presentation into narration of old news.

Rules of the room

  1. Leadership speaks last. Not "mostly last." The first regional to answer a question ends the meeting you were trying to have.

  2. You present your partner, not yourself. If you find yourself saying "and what I think is" — that's your turn being taken by someone else later.

  3. Nothing gets solved in the room. This session collects; the follow-up decides. Says so up front, so nobody leaves feeling their item disappeared.

  4. Everyone presents. No passing, no "mine's the same as his." Ten managers, ten turns.

  5. Repeats get named, not re-argued. Agreement is the finding, not the problem — four branches naming one constraint independently is the strongest signal this meeting can produce. So it gets counted, not talked out twice. Name the match, add your difference, sit down.

1. Five minutes each, or four?

Five keeps the depth and runs 75 minutes. Four runs 65. Three runs 55 and holds the under-an-hour principle this whole format was built around.

RECOMMEND

Four for the first run. Long enough for three real items, short enough that the back half of the room is still listening.

2. Who is in the room, and in what role?

Four leaders all holding the right to respond turns "leadership speaks last" into a queue, and a manager counting four executives waiting their turn presents differently than one facing two. Splitting the room into leads and observers fixes that without shrinking it.

3. Do the written notes get posted in advance, or stay with the manager?

Posting them to the Delegation Board beforehand guarantees preparation and gives leadership a read before walking in. It also means every manager writes knowing nine peers will read it, which is the exact audience problem this format exists to solve.

RECOMMEND

Announce the prompt on the DB pulse; managers bring their notes with them rather than posting. Prepared, but not performing.
